Access and everyday life

Finding suitable toilets for older adults when going out

Chcę Siku! editorial teamPublished: 27 October 20264 min read
At a glance

A suitable toilet can make a walk, shopping trip or day out easier to plan. For some older adults, a step-free entrance, clear signs and enough time to walk matter as much as distance. Start with the person's own needs and preferences, and support their independence when planning the trip.

This guide offers practical planning tips, not a diagnosis or treatment. Chcę Siku! is a toilet map, not a treatment for IBS, bladder conditions or anxiety disorders.

Start with the person, not an assumption about age

Not every older person needs more frequent toilet breaks. A useful plan takes account of the person's mobility, health, medication and comfort. If you are planning an outing with a parent, grandparent or friend, ask what would actually help instead of assuming they need assistance with everything.

Bladder leakage becomes more common with age, but it should not be dismissed as something a person must simply accept. NIDDK explains that urinary incontinence is not a routine part of getting older. A healthcare professional can help assess the cause and discuss options. A toilet map supports everyday planning; it does not replace that assessment.

Check whether the toilet is easy to reach

Look beyond the number of metres on the map. Is the entrance at street level? Are there steps, heavy doors or a steep ramp? Is the toilet close to the entrance or at the far end of a long corridor? For someone using a stick, walking frame or wheelchair, these details can matter more than a small difference in distance.

An accessibility symbol does not describe every facility. If grab rails, turning space or room for a companion are important, confirm those details with the venue. Missing information does not prove either that a feature exists or that it is absent.

Check any charge and how to pay. Coins, a card payment or a key obtained from staff are small details that can make a difference on arrival. Our accessible toilet checklist gives more questions to ask.

Build a comfortable break into the journey

Choose a toilet near your destination and check its opening hours. A park can remain open after its toilets close; a station's toilet may have different access rules from its main hall. On a longer journey, check the interchange or planned rest stop too.

Allow for the person's walking pace. A time estimate on a phone is only a guide. Look for suitable crossings, somewhere to rest and enough time to avoid rushing. The route matters as much as the facilities inside the building.

A simple backup can be useful: another nearby venue or a phone number for staff. Write the details on paper if that is easier to use while travelling. Agree on breaks together rather than making the person's decisions for them.

Do not deliberately become dehydrated to avoid toilets

Drinking too little can lead to dehydration. If bladder symptoms make fluid planning difficult, ask a healthcare professional about amounts and timing, especially if other conditions or prescribed fluid restrictions apply. NIDDK recommends individual advice about fluids, rather than restricting them to the point of dehydration.

Do not change the timing or dose of prescribed medicines on your own before a journey. If a medicine makes outings difficult to plan, discuss this with a doctor or pharmacist. Pack the supplies you normally need and follow the advice already given for your situation.

A calm, practical approach helps when travelling together: “There is a toilet on the way; we can stop there.” There is no need to comment on how often someone uses the bathroom or compare them with other people.

When should bladder symptoms be assessed?

Discuss recurring urgency, urine leakage, difficulty passing urine or frequent night-time toilet visits with a healthcare professional. Also mention if worry about toilet access is making you give up walks or social activities. It may help to bring a note of when the problem began, how it has changed and which medicines you take.

Being unable to pass urine or empty the bladder, blood in the urine, and painful urination need prompt medical attention. These warning signs are covered in the NIDDK guide to bladder-control symptoms. New symptoms should not be explained away solely by age.

Use a phone as a practical aid

Chcę Siku! lets you look up nearby toilets, read available details and see the direction to a selected place. If someone is new to the app, try the steps together at home, then on a short familiar outing. Allow time for the person to make their own choices.

After a visit, a review about the entrance or cleanliness can help others. Confirm essential facilities with the venue when the listing does not provide enough detail, because information can change. Download Chcę Siku! and see how to find and review a toilet.

This article provides general information. It does not replace individual medical advice or an assessment of a particular venue's accessibility.
